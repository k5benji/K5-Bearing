K5 Bearing

Load-Bearing

15 September 2026 · Brutalism, modern warfare, and the body

Brutalism held that a structure is honest only when you can see what bears its weight. A body keeps that rule without being asked — it cannot hide what it has carried. So what do we owe to a war now fought by systems that bear nothing, show no structure, and cannot be marked? And what does that leave for a body that must still carry, still scar, still bow?

I The Grain Left In

Brutalism was never about looking severe. That is the caricature, and it has outlived the argument. The name comes from béton brut — raw concrete — and the argument was about disclosure. When Reyner Banham tried to name what the new buildings had in common, he landed on three things: an image you could hold in the mind, structure plainly exhibited, and materials valued as found. Not dressed. Not faced. Found.

So the pine boards that held the wet concrete leave their grain in the wall, and nobody sands it off. The building keeps the record of its own making on its outside. Stand in front of one and you can read the load path with your eyes: this carries, this is carried, this only rests here. Nothing decorative stands between you and the thing holding up the roof.

That is not a metaphor about honesty. It is honesty as a structural specification. A wall that hides its work can lie about its work.

II Wolff's Law

The body keeps the same rule, except it never agreed to it.

Bone lays down material along the lines of stress it actually meets — Wolff described it in 1892, and every loaded skeleton since has confirmed it. Which means a skeleton is a diagram of what a life has asked of it. The hand thickens where it grips. The spine writes down what you carried and how often. Scar tissue is the body's admission that something got through. Callus is a ledger, and it does not take corrections.

You are board-marked concrete. The formwork is your days.

And then there is the posture that undoes all of it. Bending the knee, lowering the face to the ground: for that moment the structure carries nothing at all. Prostration is the body setting the load down and conceding it was never the thing holding everything up. The strength and the bow are the same argument made twice. You learn what weight is by taking it, and you learn what you are by putting it down.

III Standoff

Modern war spent a century moving the body away from the place where the violence lands. The word for it is standoff, and it is the governing ambition of the whole enterprise: distance, then more distance, until the distance is total.

The doctrine calls its sequence a kill chain — find, fix, track, target, engage, assess. Notice the word. A chain is, structurally speaking, a thing that transmits load along its length. This one is engineered to transmit it away. Each link is a place the weight could have been felt and was instead passed on, until it arrives somewhere no one is standing.

Pattern-of-life analysis finishes the conversion. A person becomes a signature — a set of movements matched against a template of what such movements have meant before. The body is turned into data precisely so that the decision need never touch a body. This is not a failure of the system. It is the specification.

Paul Virilio went out to the Atlantic Wall in the sixties and found the last architecture that still had to be somewhere. The bunkers are low, thick, hunched, shaped by blast the way a face is shaped by a flinch. He read them as a body's posture poured in concrete. They are the final buildings that admit what they are for.

IV A Thing That Cannot Be Marked

Now put a model in the chain.

It has no load path. Nothing in it thickens under stress. There is no formwork grain, because there was no formwork; no scar, because there is no tissue; no fatigue, because there is no material to fatigue. You cannot read its history off its surface. It has no surface, and it keeps no history — whatever weight it took was released the instant the computation ended.

This is the exact inverse of the brutalist claim. Here is a structure that exhibits nothing, and a thing that bears nothing cannot be honest, because honesty was defined as the visible bearing of weight. Worse: it cannot be held. Accountability is a grip, and a grip needs a handle. Doctrine knows this and papers over it with prepositions — a human in the loop, a human on the loop — but when the tempo outruns the nervous system, the human is not deciding. The human is signing.

The answer to what we owe such a war is therefore not a rule about machines. It is a refusal to accept a chain with no bearer at the end of it. Somebody stands under the load or the load is not being carried; it is only being moved.

V What It Leaves the Body

The easy conclusion is that the body has been made obsolete by all this, and the easy conclusion is wrong.

If cost is only real where it is legible, then the body is the last place it is legible — the one structure in the system that still cannot lie about what it has carried. That is not nostalgia. That is a job. Training under load stops being self-improvement and becomes rehearsal: practice at bearing what cannot be handed down the chain. Every honest rep is an argument against standoff.

And prayer is the other half, the counterweight. The body that has learned to carry has to learn where to set it down, because a man who can bear anything and will put nothing down is just another system with no bearer at the end of it.

One caution, so this does not come out cleaner than it is. The concrete is failing. Water gets to the rebar, the steel rusts, rust takes up more room than steel, and the honest face spalls off the building in sheets. The most truthful structures of the last century are shedding themselves onto the pavement. Honesty is not durability. It was never a promise that what bears the weight will keep bearing it — only that while it does, you will be able to see.

So: carry what is actually yours. Let it show. Put it down where it belongs.

Keep your bearing. — Kastle Five Systems
